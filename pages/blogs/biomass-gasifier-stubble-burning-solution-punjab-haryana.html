Biomass Gasifiers and Stubble Burning: A Practical Solution for Punjab and Haryana

Paddy straw that is burned in the fields every autumn can instead be collected, stored and turned into power and heat with a biomass gasifier. It is not a one-step fix, but with good collection, dry storage and a straw-ready gasifier, stubble becomes a fuel rather than a problem.

By Rai Singh Dahiya, Founder, Enersol Biopower  |  Published 12 November 2026

Key Points

  • The problem: A short gap between paddy harvest and wheat sowing pushes farmers to burn straw.
  • The opportunity: That straw is a fuel that rice mills, cold stores and factories nearby can use.
  • The chain: Collect, bale, store dry, densify, then gasify.
  • The catch: Straw is bulky and high in ash, so it needs preparation and a gasifier designed for it.
  • The reality: Gasifiers are one part of the answer, not the whole answer.

Why Stubble Gets Burned

Punjab and Haryana grow paddy in the kharif season and wheat in the rabi season. After the paddy is harvested, usually by combine, loose straw and standing stubble are left in the field. Farmers then have only a short window to clear the field and sow wheat on time.

Clearing straw takes labour, machines and money that many farmers do not have in those few weeks. Burning it takes a matchstick. That is why, despite the rules against it, fields across the region are still set on fire every autumn.

The cost is paid by everyone else. Stubble smoke adds to the air pollution that hangs over north India each winter, the heat kills useful microbes in the soil, and a resource that could have produced energy goes up in smoke. Our blog Parali se Dhua Nahi, Amdani Badhaye looks at the damage in more detail.

Two Ways to Deal with Straw

Every alternative to burning falls into one of two groups:

In-situ and ex-situ ways to manage paddy straw
Approach What happens to the straw Examples
In-situ (in the field)Mixed into the soil or left as mulchHappy Seeder, Super Straw Management System
Ex-situ (out of the field)Collected and used somewhere elseFuel for gasifiers, boilers and briquettes; biochar; biogas

Biomass gasifiers belong to the ex-situ group. They give collected straw a buyer and a use, which is what makes collection worth the effort.

How a Gasifier Turns Straw into Energy

A biomass gasifier heats dry biomass with a limited supply of air, so it turns into producer gas instead of burning away. The gas is cleaned and then used in one of two ways:

  • Power: The gas runs an engine that drives a generator.
  • Heat: The gas goes straight to a burner, dryer or furnace, replacing diesel, LPG or furnace oil. Our guide to gasifiers for process heat explains this use.

Either way, the straw is converted inside a closed reactor and the gas burns in a controlled flame, instead of smouldering across an open field.

The Straw-to-Energy Chain, Step by Step

A gasifier is only the last link. Straw reaches it through a chain, and each step has to work:

  1. Collect quickly: Straw must be gathered within the short gap before wheat sowing, usually with rakes and balers.
  2. Bale it: Baling makes loose straw easier to lift, transport and stack.
  3. Store it dry: Straw is harvested once a year but used all year, so it needs covered storage that keeps rain and ground moisture out. Keep moisture below 20%.
  4. Chop and densify: Chopping, and ideally pressing straw into briquettes or pellets, makes it denser and much easier to feed into a reactor.
  5. Gasify: A gasifier designed for agro-residue turns the prepared straw into producer gas for power or heat.

What Makes Paddy Straw a Difficult Fuel

Being honest about the challenges is what makes a project succeed. Paddy straw is harder to gasify than wood chips:

  • High ash: Straw leaves a lot of ash, so the gasifier must remove it steadily.
  • Clinker risk: Minerals in straw ash can fuse into hard lumps at high temperature, which can block the reactor if the design and operation do not allow for it.
  • Very bulky: Loose straw takes a lot of space and flows poorly, which is why densifying it helps so much.
  • Seasonal supply: The whole year's fuel arrives in a few weeks, so storage and working capital matter.

None of these rule straw out. They mean the fuel must be prepared well and the gasifier chosen for it. For a comparison with other common fuels, see Rice Husk vs Wood Chips vs Coconut Shell.

Who Can Use Straw-Based Gasifiers

The best users are close to the fields and need energy every day:

  • Rice mills: Already in the paddy belt and in need of power and heat. Read how rice mills turn waste into electricity.
  • Cold stores and warehouses: Steady power demand near farms.
  • Dryers and small factories: Process heat that can replace diesel, LPG or furnace oil.
  • Village and farmer-group clusters: Shared collection and a shared gasifier for local power or processing.

For farmers, a nearby buyer for straw turns a disposal problem into a source of extra income. The by-product char can also return to the soil. Our blog on biochar in sustainable farming explains how.

One Part of a Bigger Answer

No single technology will end stubble burning. The straw produced in Punjab and Haryana is far more than any one use can absorb. Gasifiers work best as part of a mix that also includes in-field machines, collection networks, briquetting and other users of crop residue.

What gasifiers add to that mix is a reason to collect: they create steady, local demand for straw, every day of the year, from the businesses that need energy most.

Planning a Straw-Based Gasifier Project

  • Secure the straw first: Agree collection with nearby farmers or aggregators before buying a machine.
  • Plan storage for a full year: Under cover and off the ground.
  • Decide how you will prepare it: Chopping, briquetting or pelleting.
  • Choose a gasifier designed for agro-residue, and ask the supplier to test your straw. That is one of the 10 questions to ask any gasifier manufacturer.
  • Plan ash handling and safety from day one. See our gasifier safety guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a biomass gasifier use paddy straw?

Yes, but paddy straw needs preparation. It is light, bulky and high in ash, so it is usually baled, chopped and often made into briquettes or pellets before it goes into a gasifier designed for agro-residue.

Why do farmers in Punjab and Haryana burn stubble?

After the paddy harvest there is only a short window before wheat must be sown. Clearing straw by hand or machine takes time and money, so burning it in the field is often seen as the fastest option.

How can paddy straw be turned into energy?

Straw is collected and baled after harvest, stored dry, then chopped and often densified into briquettes or pellets. A biomass gasifier converts it into producer gas, which can run an engine for electricity or a burner for process heat.

Can gasifiers alone solve stubble burning?

No single solution can. Gasifiers are one ex-situ option that turns collected straw into energy. They work best alongside in-field management, straw collection networks and other users of crop residue.

Who can use paddy straw gasifiers?

Rice mills, cold stores, dryers, small factories and village clusters close to paddy fields are well placed, because they need power or heat and can source straw locally.
